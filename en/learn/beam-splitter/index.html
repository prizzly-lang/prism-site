Beam splitters (half-silvered mirrors)

PRISM chapter 2 “Split”

A beam splitter, also called a half-silvered mirror, is an optical part that lets part of the light that hits it pass through and reflects part of it, turning one beam into two.

For
Ages 10 to 14 (extension topic)
Time
10 minutes
You need
A flashlight (torch), a sheet of clear plastic such as the lid of an empty CD case, and a sheet of white paper.
Curriculum
An extension topic: NGSS and the national curriculum for England do not name beam splitters. It builds on reflection of light, which connects to NGSS MS-PS4-2 (waves are reflected, absorbed or transmitted) and KS3 Light waves.

A small experiment

You do not need a real beam splitter. A sheet of clear plastic reflects a little light too. The darker the room, the easier this is to see.

  1. Lay the flashlight on a table so that it shines on a wall. Find the spot of light on the wall.
  2. Hold the clear sheet upright between the flashlight and the wall, turned so that the light meets it at a slant of about 45 degrees. Is the spot on the wall still there?
  3. Think of the sheet as a door standing half open. If the side facing the flashlight is turned to the left, stand the white paper to the left of the sheet; if to the right, on the right. Keep the paper about a hand's width from the sheet. Look for a second spot on the paper. It is much fainter than the one on the wall. If you cannot find it, move the paper slowly around the sheet.
  4. Turn the sheet just a little at a time. Which of the two spots moves? If a spot slides off the paper, move the paper to follow it.

Safety: use plastic, not a loose piece of glass that could break. Never shine the flashlight, or light bounced off the sheet, into anyone's eyes. Do not use a laser pointer.

What happened?

One beam became two at the sheet. Most of the light passed through and carried straight on to the wall. A small part was reflected off the surface and landed on the paper. Even something that looks perfectly clear does not let all the light through.

When you turned the sheet, only the spot on the paper moved; the spot on the wall hardly shifted. Reflected light follows the way the sheet is facing. Light that passes through keeps the direction it already had.

Glass and clear plastic reflect about 4% of the light that meets one surface nearly head-on. A sheet has a front surface and a back surface, so that makes roughly 8%, and a little more when the light arrives at a slant. That is why the second spot is faint. There is one reflection from each surface, but with a flashlight the two land almost on top of each other and look like one spot.

A window at night. You see the same thing every evening. Stand in a lit room after dark and the window shows your reflection. The glass reflects just as much in the daytime, but the daylight coming in from outside is far brighter and drowns the faint reflection out.

Real beam splitters

A beam splitter is not cloudy like tracing paper. You can see clearly through it, and it also reflects like a mirror.

A laboratory beam splitter is a glass plate with a very thin coating of metal, or with a stack of thin transparent layers. Choosing the thickness and material of the coating sets how much light is reflected and how much passes through. An even split (50:50) is the most common. Others are made uneven on purpose, such as 70:30 or 90:10. The older name, half-silvered mirror, comes from coating the glass with only a thin film of silver.

However the light is divided, no new light is made. The light that passes through, the light that is reflected and the small amount the plate absorbs always add up to the light that arrived. So each of the two beams is dimmer than the original. A 50:50 beam splitter gives two beams of about half the brightness each.

Where are they used?

  • Teleprompters: these let a news presenter read a script while looking into the camera. A slanted sheet of glass in front of the lens reflects the words from a screen below towards the presenter. The camera sits behind the glass and films the presenter with the light that passes through it.
  • One-way mirrors: a thinly coated sheet of glass stands between a bright room and a dark one. In the bright room the reflection of that room's own light is much brighter than anything coming through, so the glass looks like a mirror. In the dark room the light coming through wins, so it looks like a window. It is the window at night again.
  • Head-up displays in cars: the speed is projected onto the windshield. The glass reflects that light towards the driver while light from the road passes straight through.
  • Interferometers: split a beam in two, send the halves along different paths and bring them back together, and you can detect a tiny change in the length of either path. LIGO, which made the first detection of gravitational waves on 14 September 2015, is an interferometer of this kind with arms 4 km long.

In the game

PRISM, a light puzzle game by A Driven, introduces the beam splitter in chapter 2, “Split”. Like a mirror, it sits diagonally in one square of the grid, and a tap switches it between a “/” shape and a “\” shape. It is drawn as two parallel lines so that you can tell it from a mirror.

When a beam reaches a beam splitter, it becomes two. One carries straight on in the direction it was going. The other turns through 90 degrees, exactly as it would at a mirror of the same shape. Both keep the color of the beam that came in. A tap flips only the beam that turns; the straight one stays where it was, just as the spot on the wall stayed put when you turned the sheet.

More beams mean that one light source can light several crystals. One beam splitter makes two beams; if one of those meets a second beam splitter, there are three. The 32 boards of chapter 2 ask the same question again and again: where should this beam be divided so that it reaches every crystal? A few boards also have a wall. A wall absorbs any beam that reaches it, so the beam ends there.

The browser demo covers chapter 1, which has mirrors only. In class, ask students to sketch how many beams there will be, and where each one ends up, before they tap.

Check your understanding

A beam splitter reflects 30% of the light that hits it. Ignoring any light the plate absorbs, what percentage passes through?

70%. The light that passes through and the light that is reflected must add up to the light that arrived, so 100% - 30% = 70%. A real plate absorbs a little, so the true figure is slightly lower.

In a lit room at night you can see your reflection in the window. Why is it hard to see in the daytime?

The glass reflects the same small share of the light by day and by night. In the daytime, the light coming in through the glass from outside is far brighter, so the faint reflection goes unnoticed. At night it is dark outside and your reflection is all that is left.

In PRISM, a beam heading right meets a beam splitter shaped like “\”. What happens? And after one tap?

The beam is divided in two: one beam carries on to the right and the other turns down. After one tap the shape is “/”. The beam heading right is unchanged and the other one now turns up.

Where the game differs from real light

  • After a real beam splitter, both beams are dimmer than the beam that went in. The game does not track brightness. However many times a beam is divided, every branch lights a crystal fully.
  • Real beam splitters come in different ratios, and the ratio shifts a little with the angle and the color of the light. The plate also absorbs a little. In the game a beam splitter treats every color the same way: one beam straight on, one beam turned.
  • A real plate has thickness, so its back surface adds a second, weaker reflection, and a beam passing through at a slant comes out shifted slightly sideways. In the game the beam divides cleanly at the center of the square.
  • A real beam splitter can be set at any angle. In the game it has only the two positions “/” and “\”, like a mirror, so the turned beam always leaves at 90 degrees.
  • When real beams that have been divided are brought back together, they can make a pattern of bright and dark bands called interference, which is what an interferometer measures. There is no interference in the game.
