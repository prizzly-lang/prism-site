Color filters

PRISM chapter 5 “Filtering”

Last updated November 28, 2026 · A Driven Inc.

A color filter is a transparent material that lets through only light of its own color and mostly absorbs the rest, so white light that passes a red filter comes out red, while blue light cannot pass a red filter.

For
Ages 10 to 14
Time
10 minutes
You need
One white LED flashlight (torch), red, green and blue cellophane (yellow and cyan too if you have them), rubber bands, a white wall or sheet of white paper, a few colored objects (a red cup, a green leaf), and a dark room.
Curriculum
The Korean 2022 science curriculum and the US NGSS have no standard for color filters on their own, so treat this as an extension activity. In England, the KS3 light unit connects to the idea that the color of an object depends on how it absorbs and reflects light.

A small experiment

Cover the front of a white flashlight with red cellophane, hold it on with a rubber band, and shine it at a white wall in a dark room. The flashlight gives white light, but the spot on the wall is red. Swap the cellophane to try the steps below.

  1. Shine the flashlight through one layer of red cellophane and look at the spot. Try green and blue cellophane too.
  2. Cover the flashlight with red cellophane and a layer of green cellophane on top of it, and shine it. Compare how bright the spot is with the single-layer one. Cellophane is not perfect, so it may not go fully dark, but it is clearly dimmer.
  3. Turn off the flashlight and light the room. Hold red cellophane in front of your eye and look at a red object and a green object. Say which one looks brighter and which looks darker.
  4. (Extra) If you have yellow and cyan cellophane, stack them and shine the light on the wall. You see a color close to green.

Safety: do not shine a flashlight into anyone’s eyes. Do not use a laser pointer. Use a cool LED flashlight so the cellophane cannot heat up.

What happened

White light is a mixture of many colors (the page on dispersion of light showed a prism pulling them apart). Red cellophane lets red through and absorbs the other colors, so only red reaches the wall. The cellophane did not make red. It removed the rest.

Stacking green cellophane on top makes the spot darker for the same reason. Light that passed the red cellophane is nearly all red, and green cellophane absorbs red. Each extra layer can only take light away, never add it, so more filters means a darker result. Yellow cellophane passes red and green, and cyan cellophane passes green and blue, so stacking them leaves only green, the one color both let through.

This is the opposite direction from mixing colored light. Mixing adds colors and gets brighter, while filtering removes colors and gets darker. Mixing by taking light away is called subtractive mixing. Paint and printer ink get darker as you mix them for the same kind of reason.

Is the color of an object a similar idea?

In the third step, a red object looks bright through red cellophane and a green object looks dark. The red object reflects red light well, and the green object reflects little red light. The color of an object also comes from which colors of light it reflects and which it absorbs.

Where filters are used

  • Photography and film: a colored filter on the lens changes the mood or cuts out an unwanted color.
  • Stage lighting: a sheet of colored film in front of a white lamp makes red or blue light.
  • Traffic lights: a colored cover lets only one color of the lamp’s light out.

A scene in the game

In the light puzzle PRISM, filter pieces first appear in chapter 5 “Filtering”. A filter does not bend light. It lets it straight through but keeps only the filter’s own color. White light that passes a red filter becomes red, and blue light that reaches a red filter has nothing left, so the beam ends right there. The game has red, green and blue filters.

As in chapter 4, a crystal lights up only when the combined color is exactly its target. White light that hits a red crystal directly contains red, green and blue, so the crystal stays off. The light has to pass a red filter first, leaving only red. A filter is the piece that stops a color from reaching a crystal that must not get it.

The principle card shows a single filter, but every chapter 5 board splits white light into colors with a prism, divide beams with a beam splitter, and use filters to send just the right colors to each crystal. Filters appear in chapter 6 as well, and you can meet them in today’s light puzzles. In class, ask students what color is left after a beam passes a filter, and where the beam ends if nothing is left, before they tap anything.

Check your understanding

What comes out when white light shines on a red filter, and what happens to blue light?

White light comes out red, because only the red part is left. Blue light is absorbed by the red filter, so almost nothing comes out.

Why does stacking more color filters make the light darker?

A filter only absorbs the colors that are not its own and never makes new light. Each extra layer can only reduce the light that gets through.

In PRISM, does a red crystal light up if white light hits it directly? What does it need?

No. White light has red, green and blue together, which is different from the target red. The light has to pass a red filter first so only red is left.

Where the game differs from real light

  • Game filters are red, green or blue, and keep their own color fully while removing the rest completely. Real filters absorb a little of their own color too, so they dim it slightly, and they let a little of other colors through.
  • Light in the game has no brightness, only colors that are on or off. In reality the light gets weaker every time it passes a filter.
  • The game has no yellow or cyan filters. A real yellow filter lets red and green through together.
  • A real filter passes a fairly wide range of wavelengths, not one exact color. The game simplifies this into red, green and blue.
