Dispersion of light

PRISM chapter 3 “Dispersion”

Last updated November 21, 2026 · A Driven Inc.

Dispersion of light is the spreading of white light into its colors, because the amount that a material bends light depends on the wavelength (color), so a prism or a drop of water separates the colors.

For
Ages 10 to 14 (extension topic)
Time
10 minutes
You need
A flashlight (torch), a glass prism with a pointed edge (or a triangular clear container filled with water), a sheet of white paper, and a dark room.
Curriculum
An extension topic: England’s KS3 Light waves unit includes white light and prisms, but it does not name dispersion. NGSS MS-PS4-2 covers reflection, absorption and transmission. The page builds on reflection and refraction.

A small experiment

In a dark room, shine a flashlight onto a sheet of white paper. The light looks white. Now stand a glass prism in the path of the light (a triangular clear container of water also works). Watch how the light on the paper changes.

  1. Lay the flashlight on its side on a table so that its beam hits the white paper. Check that you have one white spot.
  2. Put the prism in the middle of the beam, so the light enters one face at an angle.
  3. Turn the prism slowly. Look at the order of the colored band that appears on the paper. If the colors are faint, make the room darker.

Safety: glass edges can be sharp, so handle with care. Do not shine the light, or light reflected from glass, into anyone’s eyes. Do not use a laser pointer.

What happened

White light is really a mixture of many colors. Wavelengths from red to violet mix together, and the result looks white. In glass, light slows down and bends, and each color bends by a slightly different amount. So light entering at the same angle leaves in a different direction for each color, and a band of colors appears on the paper.

Glass bends violet and blue light more than red light. In the colored band, violet and blue are bent the most and red the least. The order across the band is red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet.

Rainbows in raindrops

During or just after rain, stand with the sun behind you and look at the droplets in the air, and you can see a rainbow. Each droplet acts like a small prism, bending and reflecting light and separating its colors. Countless droplets do this together.

Newton’s prism experiment

In the late 1600s, Isaac Newton let sunlight enter a dark room through a small hole and passed it through a prism. A band of colors appeared on the wall. He then chose one color from the band and passed it through a second prism. That color did not split any further. Finally he gathered the colors back into one spot to make white light again.

The experiment showed that white light is a mixture of colors. Before this, many people believed that the prism was creating the colors. Newton’s results were collected in his book Opticks, published in 1704.

Where dispersion is used

  • Spectrometers: spread light by color to find out what it contains. Astronomers spread starlight in the same way and find which colors are missing, which tells them what the star is made of.
  • Camera lenses: each color focuses at a slightly different point, so photos can show colored fringes at the edges. Good lenses combine several kinds of glass to reduce this.
  • Optical fiber communication: different colors (wavelengths) travel at slightly different speeds, which can spread a signal out. Designers take this into account.

The pages on reflection of light and beam splitters covered only the direction of light. Dispersion adds that the direction can depend on the color of the same light.

A scene in the game

In the light puzzle PRISM, chapter 3 “Dispersion” introduces the prism. A prism is triangular, and the direction of its tip is set by each puzzle. Tapping it turns the prism to one of four directions.

When white light enters through the base side of the prism, it splits into three beams. Red goes straight on, green turns left, and blue turns right. White light that enters through any other face leaves as one beam without splitting. Light that already has a color, for example red-only light, also leaves as one beam.

In chapter 3, the puzzle asks you to turn the prism and choose which face the light enters, so that all three colors reach their target crystals. When the three colors must light different crystals, the question is which face the white light should enter, and from which direction. Today’s light puzzles often include a prism. In class, ask students to draw where the three beams will go before they solve the board.

Check your understanding

When white light passes through a prism, which colors bend the most and which bends the least?

Violet and blue bend the most, and red bends the least. Glass has a slightly larger refractive index for shorter wavelengths.

What happens if you shine red-only light into a prism?

It does not spread into a band. It leaves as one beam, although the glass still bends it a little. Dispersion makes a band of colors from a mixture such as white light. In PRISM, light that already has a color also leaves as one beam.

How does a rainbow form, and where is the same principle at work?

During or just after rain, sunlight bends and reflects inside the droplets in the air, and each color leaves in a slightly different direction, so it appears as a band of colors. Each droplet separates the colors like a small prism.

Where the game differs from real light

  • In real glass, dispersion is gradual and continuous: every wavelength from red to violet bends by a slightly different amount. The prism in the game splits light into only three beams: red, green and blue.
  • In the game, white light splits only when it enters through the base side. A real prism splits light through any face, and a change in the angle of entry changes the angles of the colors.
  • In the game, the split beams leave only in fixed directions (straight, left, or right), in 90-degree steps. In reality, they leave at any angle.
  • In a real prism, the colored band does not start from one point, and its brightness varies from color to color. The game does not model brightness.
