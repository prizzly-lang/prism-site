Mixing colored light

PRISM chapter 4 “Mixing”

Last updated November 26, 2026 · A Driven Inc.

Mixing colored light means overlapping lights of different colors so the eye sees a new color: overlapping two of red, green and blue light makes yellow, magenta or cyan, and all three together look white.

For
Ages 10 to 14 (the topic itself is middle school; younger students can try the experiment)
Time
10 minutes
You need
Three flashlights (torches), red, green and blue cellophane (or colored transparent film), rubber bands, a white wall or sheet of white paper, and a dark room.
Curriculum
The national standards of the United States (NGSS) and England (KS3) do not name this topic on its own, so this page works as an extension activity about color. Korea’s 2022 middle school science curriculum includes it as the composition of light.

A small experiment

Cover the front of each of three flashlights with red, green and blue cellophane, and hold it on with a rubber band. In a dark room, shine the three flashlights at a white wall. First try one at a time, then two at a time, and finally all three overlapping.

  1. Turn on one flashlight at a time. You get a red, a green and a blue spot on the wall.
  2. Shine the red and green flashlights on the same spot and look at the color. Try red with blue, and green with blue, in the same way.
  3. Overlap all three flashlights on one spot and look at the color. Prop the flashlights on books or tape about a meter from the wall so the spots are easy to overlap. Use one layer of cellophane (two layers make the light much dimmer). It is a success if the spot looks close to white, even if not perfectly white.

Safety: do not shine a flashlight into anyone’s eyes. Do not use a laser pointer. Avoid flashlights whose bulbs get hot, so the cellophane cannot heat up.

What happened

Where red and green overlap, you see yellow. Red and blue make magenta (a purplish pink), and green and blue make cyan (blue-green). All three together look close to white. Red, green and blue are called the primary colors of light.

This is the opposite of mixing paint. Paint gets darker the more you mix it, but light gets brighter the more you overlap it. Mixing by adding light together is called additive mixing. The page on dispersion of light showed white light splitting into colors; mixing gathers those colors back into white.

Is yellow light really yellow?

The yellow on the wall is not a new yellow wavelength of light. The retina of the human eye has three kinds of cone cells for color, and they respond most to red, green and blue light. When red and green light arrive together and stimulate two kinds of cone, the brain reads the signal as yellow. So the same yellow can come from one yellow wavelength, or from red and green added together.

Where mixing light is used

  • TV, phone and computer screens: they make every color by changing the brightness of tiny red, green and blue dots. A magnifying glass held to a screen shows the dots.
  • Stage lighting: lamps in red, green and blue are turned up and down to make light of any color.
  • Printers and paint work differently. Colors absorb light, so mixing them gets darker. The next concept, color filters, picks this up.

A scene in the game

In the light puzzle PRISM, chapter 4 “Mixing” puts yellow, magenta and cyan crystals at the center. Each crystal has a target color to light up. A red crystal needs red light, and a yellow crystal needs red and green together.

There are two rules. First, beams do not mix in mid air. Colors combine only when light reaches a crystal. Second, a crystal lights up only when the combined color is exactly its target. If only red reaches a yellow crystal, there is too little, so it stays off. If red, green and blue all reach it, the result is white, not yellow, so it stays off as well.

So chapter 4 asks two questions together: which colored beam goes to which crystal, and what stops a color from reaching a crystal that must not get it. Splitting a beam with a beam splitter lets one color serve two crystals, so the earlier concept is used here too. Today’s light puzzles often include colored crystals. In class, ask students what colors a crystal needs, and which colors it must not get, before they tap anything.

Check your understanding

What color do red and green light make when they overlap, and what do green and blue make?

Red and green make yellow, and green and blue make cyan (blue-green). Red and blue make magenta (a purplish pink).

How is mixing light different from mixing paint?

Paint absorbs more light the more you mix it, so it gets darker. Light gets brighter the more you overlap it. That is why all three primary colors of light make white, not a dark color.

In PRISM, does a yellow crystal light up if red, green and blue light all reach it?

No. The three colors combine into white, which is different from the target yellow. A crystal lights only when the combined color is exactly its target.

Where the game differs from real light

  • The game has only red, green and blue and their combinations: yellow, magenta, cyan and white. Real light has endless wavelengths and brightnesses.
  • Each color in the game is simply on or off, with no brightness. In reality, changing the strength of the three lights makes many more colors, such as orange, pink and lime.
  • In the game, beams do not mix in mid air and combine only at crystals. In reality too, light beams pass through each other in mid air without mixing. The combined color appears where both lights land on the same wall or enter the same eye. In the game, the crystal plays that role.
  • A game crystal lights only on an exact color match. A real eye can treat similar colors as the same color.
